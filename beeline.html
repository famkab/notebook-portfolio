Beeline

A careers service for young people up to 25 across Greater Manchester, online and offline. It began as a set of careers pages, and the work has been turning that into a service with a problem model, an evidence loop and a measurement plan underneath it.

The short version

Four things, before the detail: what was wrong, what changed around it, where it runs and how we know whether it works.

The problem

Greater Manchester rebuilt its careers offer around the and its seven . Young people were being asked to navigate that system with no clear line between what they enjoy, the subjects they pick, the pathways open to them and a real next step. The people who need it most are the least likely to be reached by a website alone.

The ground moving under it

When I started, the MBacc was a Greater Manchester framework and nothing more, so winning buy-in was part of the job: every decision had to be argued for. In July it became national policy. The question changed from whether this approach was worth backing to how a region that has already built it leads on it.

Where it runs

Ten boroughs: schools, colleges, youth organisations and community settings, plus a printed toolkit for groups with low digital access or additional needs. The audience is young people up to 25, and the adults around them.

Building it from the foundations up

Beeline existed before I arrived, as a set of careers pages: an information repository, PDFs by another name. Turning it into a service meant starting underneath it. This is the order the work went in, and the artefacts each stage produced. Click any of them to read it full size.

  1. Understanding the system before designing inside it

    Careers education in Greater Manchester is delivered by many actors with different duties, budgets and reach, and it varies by borough. I mapped the whole landscape first: who provides what, at which key stage, and where the gaps sit. It is the map the rest of the work argues against, and it is what makes a design decision defensible in a room of people who each see one part of the system.

  2. A problem model in four layers

    Rather than a feature list, the work is held against a model with four deliberate layers: the barrier or fear in a young person’s own voice, a persona who carries it, a challenge statement that opens it up, and an “I can…” statement that describes it resolved. Every phase has to name which barrier it moves, and every proposal has to trace to a challenge statement and be described as resolved through an “I can…” statement. It is what stops a good idea entering the roadmap without a need underneath it.

    The six barriers, the personas who carry them and what resolved sounds like are on the Problem Model spread.

  3. Working to a fixed political date

    The programme had a public reveal in July and an environment that moved faster than a delivery plan usually allows. Working to that meant deciding early what had to be right for the reveal and what could be learned afterwards, and holding the line on the difference. It also meant challenging some conventional thinking about what a careers service is for, because the honest answer to what young people needed was not always the thing that was easiest to show.

  4. A design assurance loop, before anything was built

    Partner feedback, user research and youth voice all land in one log, and each item becomes a fix, a research question or a decision with a trade-off recorded against it. Decisions are argued at in front of everyone they affect, so the reasoning survives the people who made it.

    Four trails from a fear to a shipped decision are on Decisions & Metrics.

  5. A measurement model that can say whether it worked

    Programme metrics answer whether the service is reaching people. Product metrics answer whether it is working once they arrive. Five Learning Moments carry the change story between them, and each metric holds a maturity position: named, defined, baselined, trended. A metric that is only named is shown as only named, so the board reads a measurement plan, with its gaps visible.

  6. A roadmap with Learn windows as well as Build windows

    Five phases to 2030, aligned to the MBacc years, with learning windows written into the plan and given their own time. A phase that only builds cannot tell you whether the last thing worked, and a service for young people that never checks becomes an information repository again.

  7. First versions, in testing

    The Discovery quiz online and offline are v1s in use and under test, not finished products. The blueprint below is how they hold together: what a young person does, which touchpoint carries it, what it is supposed to feel like, who supports them, and which Learning Moment each phase is meant to move.

  8. As-is and to-be, so it can be argued about

    The same journey drawn twice: how it runs now, and how it should run once the decisions land. Two maps side by side make a trade-off discussable by people who were not in the research. That is most of the people who decide whether it gets built.
