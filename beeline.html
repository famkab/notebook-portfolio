Beeline

A careers service for young people up to 25 across Greater Manchester, online and offline. It began as a set of gateway pages, and the work has been turning that into a service with a problem model, an evidence loop and a measurement plan underneath it.

The short version

Four things, in brief: what was wrong, what changed around it, where it runs and how we know whether it works.

The problem

Greater Manchester added the and its seven to its careers offer, in recognition of the Greater Manchester Strategy’s ambition to give every young person in the region a clear line of sight to high quality jobs. Young people were being asked to navigate that system with no clear line between what they enjoy, the subjects they pick, the pathways open to them and a real next step. In addition to that, the people who need it most are the least likely to be reached by a website alone.

The ground moving under it

When I started, the MBacc was a Greater Manchester framework and nothing more, so winning buy-in was part of the job: every decision had to be argued for. In July it became national policy. The question changed from whether this approach was worth backing to how a region that has already built it leads on it.

Where it runs

Ten boroughs: schools, colleges, youth organisations and community settings, plus an offline, “unplugged” toolkit for groups with low digital access or additional needs. The audience is young people up to 25, and the trusted adults around them.

Building it from the foundations up

Beeline existed before I arrived, as a set of gateway pages: an information repository, PDFs by another name. Turning it into a service meant starting underneath the surface. This is the order the work went in, and the artefacts each stage produced. Click any of them to read it full size.

  1. Understanding the system before designing inside it

    Careers education in Greater Manchester is delivered by many actors with different duties, budgets and reach, and it varies by borough. I mapped the whole landscape first. Year 7 to Year 13 runs across the top, and nine bands run down the side, from what the system is doing to what we build and where it reaches. Reading down a column shows one key stage. Reading across a row shows how one thing changes as a young person gets older.

    I map a system like this to find the gaps and to build my own domain knowledge. It earns its keep twice: I can walk a stakeholder through the journey whatever their background, and one session over it with developers before the spec is written saves a lot of confusion later. People who work in the system have marked it up since, and every cell carries found or inferred, so the gaps in my own evidence stay visible.

    A sample Four of the nine bands, at all three key stages, are on Service Landscape.

  2. A problem model in four layers

    Rather than a feature list, the work is held against a model with four deliberate layers: the barrier or fear in a young person’s own voice, a persona who carries it, a challenge statement that opens it up, and an “I can…” statement that describes it resolved. Every phase has to name which barrier it moves, and every proposal has to trace to a challenge statement and be described as resolved through an “I can…” statement. A good idea with no need underneath it is a waste, and often the wrong thing to try.

    Three of the six barriers, the personas who carry them and what resolved sounds like are on the Problem Model spread.

  3. Working to a fixed political date

    The programme had a public reveal in July and an environment that moved faster than a delivery plan usually allows. Working to that meant deciding early what had to be right for the reveal and what could be learned afterwards, and holding the line on the difference. It also meant challenging some conventional thinking about what a careers service is for, because the honest answer to what young people needed was not always the thing that was easiest to show. Sometimes surfacing that need from young people is the work in itself.

  4. A design assurance loop, before anything was built

    Partner feedback, user research and youth voice all land in one log, and each item becomes a fix, a research question or a decision with a trade-off recorded against it. Decisions are argued at in front of the key team members they affect.

    Two trails from a fear to a shipped decision are on Decisions & Metrics.

  5. A measurement model that can say whether it worked

    Programme metrics answer whether the service is reaching people, and sometimes give us a bit of a qualitative picture. Product metrics answer whether it is working once they arrive. Five Learning Moments carry the change story between them, and each metric holds a maturity position: named, defined, baselined, trended. A metric that is only named is shown as only named, so the board reads a measurement plan, with its gaps visible. Numbers are never shown without the picture behind them, or the qualitative data that supports them.

  6. A roadmap with Learn windows as well as Build windows

    Five phases to 2030, aligned to the MBacc years, with learning windows written into the plan and given their own time. I added those windows. Time to learn from the people we are building for is a good investment, and good practice. A phase that only builds cannot tell you whether the last thing worked, and we want a service young people actually use.

  7. First versions, in testing

    The Discovery quiz online and offline are v1s in use and under test. They are in Launch & Learn, the part of the year where we learn from them being out in the wild. The blueprint below is how they hold together: what a young person does, which touchpoint carries it, what it is supposed to feel like, who supports them, and which Learning Moment each phase is meant to move.

    The same journey drawn twice: how it runs now, with the pains surfaced, and how it should run once they are resolved.
