We mapped how the Discovery toolkit felt to young people, phase by phase, across many sessions in schools, colleges, youth settings and at regional events. Their confidence dips while they’re working out the game, then spikes when they figure it out. Some of that dip is the point. The design question I took to Show & Tells was how much struggle to keep, and which kind.

How this compares with the online journey is on Beeline Unplugged.

Two kinds of struggle

The Show & Tell reflections kept returning to play: instant gratification versus delayed gratification. The answer wasn’t to flatten the curve. It was to take out the friction that’s about the game, and protect the friction that’s about them.

Remove: friction about the game
  • Working out the rules alone (“we sort of had to make up rules”)
  • Not knowing how many cards to pick
  • A scoring model that read as arbitrary
  • A cold drop-off from tactile cards to static web pages
Keep: friction about them
  • Not knowing yet what sounds like you, and talking it through with peers
  • Self-reflection before any gateway label is applied
  • A result that differs from your plan, with an adult there to open it up
  • Changing direction later, treated as the point, not a failure

What we decided at Show & Tells, and what it cost

What’s a Show & Tell? In UK public-sector delivery teams, a Show & Tell is a standing session where the team shows work in progress, not polished work, with the evidence behind it. Anyone with a stake can come: the programme team, the digital team, partners, and colleagues from other services. The point is to argue the trade-offs out loud and leave with a decision recorded.
