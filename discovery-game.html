Beeline Unplugged

The Discovery Game: a deck of cards that runs the Beeline discovery logic without a screen. Why it was worth building, what six rounds of field testing showed, and what the first round could not yet tell us.

The argument

Beeline is a digital service. Asking a public body to spend money printing cards means showing what the website cannot do on its own. Three programme problems carried the case.

The home–school connection

A young person in a busy assembly will not save a link or scan a code they have no reason to care about yet. Something they can carry out of the room, and show a parent, moves the conversation past the school gate.

Access and inclusion

Built on Universal Design for Learning: give people more than one way to act and express. Useful for younger learners, for learners, and for anyone who finds a careers website intimidating before they find it useful.

Channel resilience

A large share of direct engagement came through social media. Restrictions on young people's use of it are a live policy question, so a physical route into the service is a hedge against losing a channel we do not control.

The pattern came from computer science education, where unplugged activities are long established through Barefoot Computing, the Raspberry Pi Foundation and the National Centre for Computing Education. I know that field from the inside: I have built digital learning products, learned to code, and taught science. Bringing a method that already works next door into careers education is how the case was made, and knowing why it works there is what let me adapt it rather than copy it.

What it is

Eighteen prompt cards, a scoring model printed on their backs, seven gateway cards, a tally chart and a facilitation guide. Two to three minutes with one young person, or a whole class with one adult.

  • 18 prompt cardsSix Interests (“I enjoy…”), six Skills (“I'm good at…”) and six Ambitions (“I want a job that…”). Deliberately broad and youth-readable: helping people, making and fixing, solving problems, being creative, leading, organising.
  • Scoring on the backThree points for a clear fit, one for a related one, nothing otherwise, with a cap of two gateways sharing any score. The model is printed where the young person can see it, so the result is something they can argue with.
  • 7 gateway cardsEach one carries recognisable roles and why the sector matters locally, so a stops being an abstract label.
  • Tally chartFilled in by hand, taken home, and carrying the QR code back to the digital service.
  • Facilitation guideTwo cards. The opening line is “You don't need to be a careers expert”, because the adult in the room usually is not one.

It is framed throughout as exploration, not job-matching. No right answers, nothing closed off, and no premature narrowing at 14.

Six settings, to find out whether it worked

Round one ran across six public events between February and May 2026, each one chosen to stress a different part of it: crowd and noise, a quiet corner, younger learners, older learners, families, and a room with employers in it. Nothing was printed at volume until those rooms had been through it.

The bridge works at home, not in the room

On the one day the QR tracking was live, the general link printed on the take-home tally chart was scanned about twice as often as all the gateway-specific codes on the panels combined. People scanned the thing in their pocket later, not the board in front of them. That finding changed where the codes go.

What the sessions taught us

  • PaceTwo to three minutes per young person, and about four per pair of facilitators before conversation quality dropped.
  • PeersGroups of friends argued about which cards fitted each other. The best part came after the activity finished.
  • NoiseA quiet corner produced the deepest engagement of the round.
  • ReadingSEND groups needed the cards read aloud, which the format allows.
  • ConfidenceWhen the tally matched a plan someone already had, it read as permission.
  • EmployersLaid out with barely any explanation, employers picked the cards up and used them to explain their own sectors.

What we could not measure

The report to the board said this in as many words: an early operational review, not an evaluation, and not to be used for claims about impact or demand.

  • Age, setting and SEND status were observed, not collected, so no demographic breakdown holds.
  • No before-and-after measure, so no claim about confidence, clarity or intent.

The tracking failure became the first operational priority for round two. A measure with a known hole in it is worth more to a board than a number nobody can defend.

Two shapes of the same journey

The offline and online halves run the same discovery logic and behave nothing alike. The blueprint holds five phases, from arrival to re-exploration, and each one favours a different half. Designing both meant being honest about which.

PhaseOffline, with the cardsOnline, on the service
Arrival An adult sets it up, so the entry cost is a sentence. The pack is sitting on a table and someone picks it up. They arrive alone and have to decide it is worth their time before anything has happened.
Active discovery Cards, tally, panels and the person next to you, all live at once. Order is not fixed and people move between them. One question at a time, in the order we chose. Clean, quick, and the same for everyone.
The reveal The result lands in a room with someone in it. A surprising score becomes a conversation. The result lands on a screen with nobody there. A surprising score can read as a verdict.
The bridge Depends on a code being scanned later, often at home, from a chart in a pocket. Already there. The next page is one tap away.
Coming back Needs the pack again, and usually the adult again. Costs nothing to return to, at any hour, as many times as they like.
What the physical half is for

A whole group with one adult, no device and no signal, read aloud where it needs to be, and an object that leaves the building. Peer talk is where the best sessions happened.

What the digital half is for

One clean path, instrumented end to end. It scales at no extra cost per person, holds more depth than a card, and tells us where people stopped.

What each one costs you

The pack costs money per person and depends on the adult in the room. The service is free to repeat, and it reaches the people who arrive.

The design question was where the handover sits, and what has to survive it.

The Next Steps Guide gave a strong sense of completion and agency where people reached it. Anyone who stopped short of it lost that, which argues for putting the guide at the end of the unplugged experience too rather than only online.

Designing it to cost less

Public money, so the cheapest version that still worked was part of the design rather than a constraint applied afterwards. Four things brought the cost per young person down.

  • Bulk, not kitsSupplier minimums mean a single kit assembled to order costs many times what the same kit costs inside a full run. Ordering every component in one print run and assembling the kits ourselves moved the cost per contact from prohibitive to routine.
  • Four configurationsEach sized to where it goes. A summit giveaway is a deck and a guide. A school kit is boxed so it survives a term and gets reused. An event kit carries the panels and the stickers that only a staffed stand needs. Nothing pays for materials it will not use.
  • One deck, many young peopleThe tally chart is downloadable, so a class of thirty needs one deck and thirty printed sheets. That decision alone changed what a school has to be given.
  • A v1, not the full orderThe recommendation was a smaller first batch to early events and a pilot group, with the tracked codes running, then a v2 once we knew where the friction was. Money follows evidence instead of arriving ahead of it.

The specification argued for durability where it earns its keep and against it where it does not: rounded corners and printed boxes for the decks that live in classrooms, plain banding for the ones handed out at a summit and used once.
