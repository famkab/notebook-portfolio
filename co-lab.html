CO/LAB

A lab technology nobody outside the lab could use. Nine months of design thinking with the Italian Institute of Technology ended with an onboarding service for transferring science out of the lab, presented at the SUGAR Expo in San Francisco.

The challenge

What they had

The Italian Institute of Technology’s artificial leaf: a system that imitates photosynthesis, taking sunlight, water and carbon dioxide and converting them into useful molecules. At technology readiness level 4, it still lived in the lab.

The brief

“How might we develop an innovative service based on the artificial photosynthesis technology, and create a business opportunity out of it?” Six students from the University of Bologna and the Université Côte d’Azur, across service design, engineering, architecture, economics and education.

The u-turn

We spent the first phase looking for an application: chimneys, cement, fashion, buildings, transport, plastic upcycling, agriculture. We picked plastics, researched it hard, prototyped against it, and then turned the challenge around. The real problem was one step earlier.

What we were asked

Find a market for the leaf, then build a business case around it.

→
What we designed instead

Fix how the technology is explained and handed over in the first place. Science communication and onboarding are what decide whether a transfer happens at all.

What I made

  • Journey maps, as-is and to-beHow a knowledge transfer office actually moves a technology toward industry, and where it stalls.
  • Value proposition mapsTwo of them: one for the institute, one for the company on the other side of the handover, because they want different things.
  • System map and service blueprintWho does what, in what order, and what has to exist backstage for the front stage to work.
  • Business model canvasHow the service would pay for itself, and who pays.
  • The toolboxThe front end and the back end of the prototype: a guided tool, the interface around it, and a 3D model of the leaf so a non-scientist can see what it does.

How it was tested

We interviewed researchers and business developers, prototyped early with a rough version, and ran the tool against a real case, a company working on the same kind of technology. The last checks asked whether it held up for a different technology, not just this one.

What I took from it

The brief you are given is not always the problem you should solve. Turning the challenge around six months in cost us time, and it was still the right call.

Designing for scientists and business developers taught me to hold two value propositions at once, which is the same muscle I use now between a combined authority, a school and a young person.

It is also where I learned that a prototype’s job is to make an abstract technology touchable, whether that’s a 3D model of a leaf or a pack of printed cards.
